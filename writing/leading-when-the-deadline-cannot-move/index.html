Leading when the deadline cannot move

Most deadlines in corporate life are softer than they look. They were set by someone, which means they can be reset by someone. Experienced teams know this, and it quietly shapes how they behave when a plan comes under pressure.

The LIBOR transition was different. When the regulators announced that the interest rate benchmarks underpinning trillions of dollars of contracts would cease at the end of 2021, they set a date that no bank, no matter how large, could negotiate. I led the technology delivery for that transition across the sales and trading businesses of a global investment bank. It remains the clearest lesson in leadership I have had.

The first thing a truly fixed date changes is honesty. When a deadline can slip, scope debates drag on because everyone believes time can be found later. When it cannot slip, prioritisation becomes real. We had to say plainly what would be done, what would not be done and what risk we were accepting as a result. Writing those choices down and getting senior stakeholders to agree them felt uncomfortable at the time. It was the most valuable thing we did.

The second change is in how you treat the plan. On a programme of that size the plan is not a promise, it is a working hypothesis that will be wrong somewhere. The job of the leader is to find out where it is wrong as early as possible. We learned to reward people for surfacing bad news quickly and to treat a slipped milestone reported early as good behaviour rather than failure.

The third change is what it does to communication upwards. Boards and regulators did not need optimism from us. They needed an accurate picture of where we stood, what worried us and what we were doing about it. Once we settled into that rhythm, trust followed, and with trust came the freedom to run the programme properly rather than defensively.

Very few leaders will run a benchmark transition, but most will face a moment when the room believes a date can move and reality says otherwise. The lesson I carry into my coaching work is that the immovable deadline was never really the problem. It was the thing that finally made the honest conversations happen. The best leaders I know find ways to have those conversations without waiting for a regulator to force them.
