Why I left a career in technology to coach

For more than thirty years I built and ran technology in the financial markets. I worked on the systems that moved a trading floor onto screens, led global teams through regulatory change and finished my executive career managing the migration of a major bank onto cloud infrastructure. It was demanding, absorbing work and I was fortunate to do it alongside some remarkable people.

Somewhere in those years I noticed a pattern. The moments I kept returning to in my own mind were rarely the technical wins. They were conversations. A colleague in my office doorway trying to decide whether to take a role that frightened them. A talented manager who had lost confidence after a project went wrong and needed help finding it again. A senior leader rehearsing a difficult message before walking into a room to deliver it.

Those conversations were never in my job description. They were the part of the job I would have done for free.

When I stepped back from full-time executive work I gave myself the time to ask what I actually wanted the next stage to be. The honest answer was that I wanted more of those conversations and I wanted to be properly good at them. Experience is valuable but it is not the same thing as skill. So I trained with Meyler Campbell, took the craft seriously and discovered that coaching done well is far more disciplined than the friendly chat it is sometimes mistaken for.

What surprised me most in training was how much I had to unlearn. A career in delivery teaches you to solve problems quickly and to be the person in the room with the answer. Coaching asks for something different. The discipline is to hold back your own answer long enough for the other person to find theirs, which is usually better suited to their situation than yours would have been.

I still love technology and I still follow the industry closely. But the work I do now feels less like a change of direction than an arrival. It turns out the thread that ran through the whole career was never the systems. It was the people building them.
